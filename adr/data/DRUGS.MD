# Diphenhydramine

## Overview

Diphenhydramine, sold under the brand name Benadryl among others, is a
first-generation antihistamine and sedative. It is a first-generation
H1-antihistamine (it blocks histamine) with sedative effects, and is
also a potent anticholinergic. It is mainly used to treat allergies,
insomnia, and symptoms of the common cold, and is less commonly used for
tremors in parkinsonism and nausea. Although generally considered
sedating, diphenhydramine can cause paradoxical central nervous system
stimulation in some individuals, particularly at higher doses, which may
manifest as agitation, anxiety, or restlessness rather than sedation.


## Mechanism

Diphenhydramine, while traditionally known as an antagonist, acts
primarily as an inverse agonist of the histamine H1 receptor. It
crosses the blood-brain barrier and inversely agonizes H1 receptors
centrally, which causes drowsiness. It is also a potent antimuscarinic
(a competitive antagonist of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors), and at
high doses this activity can cause anticholinergic syndrome. It also
acts as an intracellular sodium channel blocker, responsible for its
local anesthetic properties, and has been shown to inhibit reuptake of
serotonin. 

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

The most prominent side effects are dizziness and sleepiness.

Diphenhydramine is a potent anticholinergic agent and a potential
deliriant in higher doses. This activity is responsible for the side
effects of dry mouth and throat, increased heart rate, pupil dilation,
urinary retention, constipation, and, at high doses, hallucinations or
delirium. Other side effects include motor impairment (ataxia), flushed
skin, blurred vision at nearpoint owing to lack of accommodation
(cycloplegia), abnormal sensitivity to bright light (photophobia),
sedation, difficulty concentrating, short-term memory loss, visual
disturbances, irregular breathing, dizziness, irritability, itchy skin,
confusion, increased body temperature (in general, in the hands or feet),
temporary erectile dysfunction, and excitability, and although it can be
used to treat nausea, higher doses may cause vomiting. Diphenhydramine in
overdose may occasionally result in QT prolongation.

Some individuals experience an allergic reaction to diphenhydramine in
the form of hives.

Conditions such as restlessness or akathisia can worsen from increased
levels of diphenhydramine, especially with recreational dosages. Normal
doses of diphenhydramine, like other first-generation antihistamines, can
also make symptoms of restless legs syndrome worse. As diphenhydramine is
extensively metabolized by the liver, caution should be exercised when
giving the drug to individuals with hepatic impairment.

Anticholinergic use later in life is associated with an increased risk
for cognitive decline and dementia among older people. These findings
have resulted in a blanket association between diphenhydramine and
increased risk of dementia in older patients. However, studies have found
only certain classes of anticholinergic agents associated with this risk.
A large, nested case-control study, published in JAMA in 2019, found that
associations were strongest for the anticholinergic antidepressants,
bladder antimuscarinics, antipsychotics, and antiepileptic drugs, and
that there were no significantly increased risks for antihistamines.
Drowsiness, memory loss, confusion, dry mouth or constipation may occur
in elderly people. 

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

The elimination half-life of diphenhydramine has not been fully
elucidated, but appears to range between 2.4 and 9.3 hours in healthy
adults. A 1985 review of antihistamine pharmacokinetics found that the
elimination half-life ranged between 3.4 and 9.3 hours across five
studies, with a median of 4.3 hours. A subsequent 1990 study found the
elimination half-life was 5.4 hours in children, 9.2 hours in young
adults, and 13.5 hours in the elderly. A 1998 study found a half-life of
4.1 ± 0.3 hours in young men, 7.4 ± 3.0 hours in elderly men, 4.4 ± 0.3
hours in young women, and 4.9 ± 0.6 hours in elderly women. 

## Dosing Considerations

According to American Geriatric Society guidelines, diphenhydramine
possesses strong anticholinergic properties and should generally be
avoided in older adults, though it may still be used under close
monitoring for acute allergic reactions. The usual adult oral dose is
25-50 mg three to four times daily, with a maximum of 300 mg/day; no
separate lower geriatric starting dose is established in general
prescribing references the way it is for diazepam, since the guideline
recommendation is avoidance rather than dose reduction. Given that
elimination half-life roughly doubles from young adult to elderly
patients, a standard adult dose taken at bedtime can still be
pharmacologically active well into the next day in an older patient.
[StatPearls, Drugs.com]

## Interactions

Alcohol may increase the drowsiness caused by diphenhydramine.
Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) prolong and intensify the
anticholinergic effect of antihistamines. 

## Monitoring

Given the dementia-risk nuance above, ongoing monitoring should focus on
acute anticholinergic symptoms actually documented for this drug class
confusion, memory changes, dry mouth, constipation, and urinary
retention  rather than treating long-term dementia risk as an
established antihistamine-specific concern. Cognitive status and fall
risk should be checked at each visit for any elderly patient on
ongoing diphenhydramine use, given the prolonged half-life in this
population.

## Alternatives

Since the American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria lists
diphenhydramine as a medication to avoid in seniors, second-generation
antihistamines are generally preferred: loratadine (10 mg once daily),
cetirizine (10 mg once daily), or fexofenadine (60 mg twice daily, or
180 mg once daily), each carrying substantially lower anticholinergic
and sedative burden than first-generation agents like diphenhydramine.
[Ubie/AGS Beers Criteria; Drugs.com]

## Urgent Signs

Diphenhydramine is one of the most commonly misused over-the-counter
drugs in the United States. Overdose symptoms may include: abdominal
pain, abnormal speech, acute megacolon, anxiety/nervousness, coma,
delirium, disorientation, dissociation, dysphoria, extreme drowsiness,
flushed skin, hallucinations, heart palpitations, inability to urinate,
motor disturbances, muscle spasms, seizures, severe dizziness, severe
mouth and throat dryness, tremors, and vomiting.

Acute poisoning can be fatal, leading to cardiovascular collapse and
death in 2-18 hours, and is generally treated using a symptomatic and
supportive approach. Diphenhydramine can block the delayed rectifier
potassium channel and, as a consequence, prolong the QT interval, leading
to cardiac arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes. No specific antidote
for diphenhydramine toxicity is known, but the anticholinergic syndrome
has been treated with physostigmine for severe delirium or tachycardia.
Benzodiazepines may be administered to decrease the likelihood of
psychosis, agitation, and seizures in people who are prone to these
symptoms. 

# Amitriptyline

## Overview

Amitriptyline is a tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) that blocks the reuptake
of both serotonin and norepinephrine. It is FDA-approved for depression,
and is also commonly prescribed off-label for neuropathic pain, migraine
prophylaxis, and insomnia -- uses that make it likely to appear in geriatric
medication lists for reasons beyond depression treatment.

## Mechanism

Amitriptyline's harm profile in older adults comes from two mechanisms
layered on top of each other: anticholinergic, antihistaminic, and
alpha-adrenergic blocking effects, plus direct effects on cardiac
conduction. This dual mechanism distinguishes it from a pure antihistamine
like diphenhydramine -- the anticholinergic burden overlaps, but
amitriptyline adds a cardiac risk pathway diphenhydramine does not carry.

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

A meta-analysis of 23 randomized controlled trials found anticholinergic
adverse drug reactions occurred at a far higher rate with amitriptyline
than placebo (odds ratio 7.41), with dry mouth, drowsiness, and sedation
the most frequently reported effects; this burden was not dose-dependent,
meaning even low doses carry meaningful risk.

Peripheral effects include tachycardia, urinary retention, constipation,
dry mouth, blurred vision, and exacerbation of narrow-angle glaucoma.
Central effects include cognitive impairment, psychomotor slowing,
confusion, sedation, and delirium. On the cardiac side, toxicity symptoms
include tachycardia, hypotension, conduction abnormalities, and QTc
prolongation.

Beyond the anticholinergic and cardiac effects, amitriptyline lowers the
seizure threshold in a dose-dependent way, with a reported seizure rate of
roughly 1-4% at daily doses of 250-450 mg. It can also produce mild,
usually transient elevations in liver enzymes, and carries an association
with increased bone fracture risk, both of which matter for older
patients who are more likely to have baseline hepatic impairment or
osteoporosis risk.

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

A controlled study in elderly depressed patients found amitriptyline
produced a transient heart rate increase, a markedly increased orthostatic
blood pressure drop without a compensatory heart rate response, and
significant impairment of myocardial conduction (PQ/QRS prolongation) even
at standard dosing. This orthostatic effect compounds with sedation to
create fall risk through two independent mechanisms at once.

Older adults also have reduced hepatic metabolism and slower intestinal
transit, which together produce higher plasma concentrations for a given
oral dose compared to younger patients -- meaning the same dose carries
proportionally more risk in an elderly patient.

## Dosing Considerations

The manufacturer's prescribing information states elderly patients may be
at increased risk for falls, and recommends starting on low doses with
close observation. Guideline-based elderly-specific dosing recommends
starting around 10 mg/day, well below the standard adult starting dose of
25 mg/day, with maintenance doses split into smaller increments (e.g.,
10 mg three times daily plus 20 mg at bedtime) for patients who do not
tolerate higher single doses.

Because anticholinergic ADRs are not dose-dependent, exceeding a "safe"
dose does not proportionally increase anticholinergic risk, but it does
increase cardiac conduction risk and seizure risk, both of which are
dose-related.

## Interactions

Amitriptyline has many potential drug interactions that increase the risk
of arrhythmias and serotonin syndrome. Notably, amitriptyline is
metabolized in part through CYP2C19, and omeprazole -- itself metabolized
by and an inhibitor of CYP2C19 -- can raise amitriptyline levels enough to
precipitate delirium when the two are taken together, a combination worth
flagging explicitly given how common proton pump inhibitor use is in
geriatric patients. Its anticholinergic mechanism also directly overlaps
with diphenhydramine's, so a patient on both drugs faces compounded
anticholinergic burden rather than two independent risks.

## Monitoring

Given the cardiac findings above, patients with a history of cardiac
problems or over age 50 should have a baseline ECG to establish baseline
QTc before starting therapy. Ongoing monitoring should include heart rate,
blood pressure (specifically orthostatic changes), ECG conduction
intervals, and periodic amitriptyline serum concentration checks when
toxicity or non-adherence is suspected -- in addition to the standard
anticholinergic symptom watch (confusion, constipation, urinary retention)
used for antihistamines.

## Alternatives

For depression or neuropathic pain in older adults, SSRIs or
non-anticholinergic agents are generally preferred (e.g., sertraline,
starting at 25 mg once daily, the lower end of the standard starting
range). If a TCA is clinically necessary, secondary amines such as
nortriptyline (geriatric dosing: 30-50 mg/day in divided doses, per
Drugs.com) carry lower anticholinergic burden than tertiary amines like
amitriptyline, and are also not metabolized by CYP2C19 in the same way,
avoiding the omeprazole interaction noted above.

## Urgent Signs

Amitriptyline toxicity becomes clinically significant above roughly
5 mg/kg and can be life-threatening. Cardiac symptoms include tachycardia,
hypotension, conduction abnormalities, and QTc prolongation; neurological
symptoms progress from sedation to seizure to coma; anticholinergic
symptoms include dilated pupils, dry mouth, absent bowel sounds, and
urinary retention. Any of these in combination, especially in a patient
on amitriptyline with a recent dose change, warrants immediate emergency
evaluation rather than routine follow-up.


# Diazepam

## Overview

Diazepam, sold under the brand name Valium among others, is a medication
of the benzodiazepine family that acts as an anxiolytic. It is used to
treat a range of conditions, including anxiety, seizures, alcohol
withdrawal syndrome, muscle spasms, insomnia, and restless legs syndrome.
Common side effects include sleepiness and trouble with coordination.
Long-term use can result in tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal
symptoms on dose reduction, and abrupt stopping after long-term use can
be potentially dangerous. Its mechanism of action works by increasing the
effect of the neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA).


## Mechanism

Benzodiazepines are positive allosteric modulators of the GABA type A
receptors (GABAA), which are ligand-gated chloride-selective ion channels
activated by GABA, the major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain.
The binding of benzodiazepines promotes GABA binding, increasing chloride
ion influx and hyperpolarizing the neuron's membrane potential, which
reduces arousal of the cortical and limbic systems. Different receptor
subunit compositions mediate different effects: receptors containing the
alpha-1 subunit mediate sedative, anterograde amnesic, and partly
anticonvulsive effects; receptors containing alpha-2 mediate anxiolytic
and myorelaxant effects. 

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Benzodiazepines, such as diazepam, can cause anterograde amnesia,
confusion, and sedation. The elderly are more prone to diazepam's
confusion, amnesia, ataxia, hangover symptoms, and falls. Diazepam and
other benzodiazepines can produce anterograde amnesia, but not retrograde
amnesia, meaning information learned before using benzodiazepines is not
impaired. Short-term benzodiazepine use does not lead to tolerance, and
the elderly are more sensitive to them. After stopping benzodiazepines,
cognitive problems may last at least six months. Benzodiazepines may also
cause or worsen depression. Sedatives and sleeping pills, including
diazepam, have been associated with an increased risk of death.

Diazepam has a range of side effects common to most benzodiazepines,
including suppression of REM sleep and slow wave sleep, impaired motor
function, impaired coordination, impaired balance, dizziness, and reflex
tachycardia. Less commonly, paradoxical reactions can occur, including
nervousness, irritability, excitement, worsening of seizures, insomnia,
muscle cramps, and in some cases rage and violence  these adverse
reactions are more likely to occur in children and the elderly. Diazepam
in doses of 5 mg or more causes significant deterioration in alertness
performance combined with increased feelings of sleepiness. 

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

The elderly metabolise benzodiazepines much more slowly than younger
adults, and are also more sensitive to the effects of benzodiazepines,
even at similar blood plasma levels. The elimination half-life of
diazepam and its active metabolite desmethyldiazepam increases
significantly in the elderly, which may result in prolonged action, as
well as accumulation of the drug during repeated administration.


## Dosing Considerations

Doses of diazepam are recommended to be about half of those given to
younger people in elderly patients, and treatment is limited to a
maximum of two weeks. Long-acting benzodiazepines such as diazepam are
not recommended for the elderly. Elderly and very ill patients can
experience apnea or cardiac arrest, and concomitant use of other central
nervous system depressants increases this risk  the smallest possible
effective dose is generally used for this group. 

## Interactions

Diazepam increases the central depressive effects of alcohol, other
hypnotics/sedatives (e.g., barbiturates), other muscle relaxants, certain
antidepressants, sedative antihistamines, opioids, and antipsychotics, as
well as anticonvulsants such as phenobarbital, phenytoin, and
carbamazepine. Cimetidine, omeprazole, oxcarbazepine, ticlopidine,
topiramate, ketoconazole, itraconazole, disulfiram, fluvoxamine,
isoniazid, erythromycin, probenecid, propranolol, imipramine,
ciprofloxacin, fluoxetine, and valproic acid prolong the action of
diazepam by inhibiting its elimination. Alcohol in combination with
diazepam may cause a synergistic enhancement of the hypotensive
properties of both. Rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, and phenobarbital
increase the metabolism of diazepam, decreasing drug levels and effects.


## Monitoring

Elderly and very ill patients should be monitored for apnea or cardiac
arrest, particularly when other central nervous system depressants are
used concomitantly. Given the significantly increased fall risk, ongoing
monitoring should track gait, balance, and cognitive status (confusion,
amnesia) throughout therapy, not only at initiation.

## Alternatives

For elderly patients, non-benzodiazepine approaches to anxiety, insomnia,
or muscle spasm are generally preferred given the fall risk, cognitive
effects, and dependence potential: buspirone (initial dose 7.5 mg twice
daily, increased by 5 mg/day at 2-3 day intervals as needed; caution
advised in renal impairment, not recommended in severe impairment) for
anxiety, for example. If a benzodiazepine is necessary, shorter-acting
agents without active metabolites, at the lowest effective dose, are
typically preferred over long-acting agents like diazepam in older
adults. 

## Urgent Signs

Diazepam can also be dangerous in geriatric patients owing to a
significantly increased risk of falls. An individual who has consumed
too much diazepam typically displays drowsiness, mental confusion,
hypotension, impaired motor function, impaired reflexes, impaired
coordination, impaired balance, dizziness, or coma within approximately
four hours of a suspected overdose. The antidote for benzodiazepine
overdose is flumazenil, used only in cases with severe respiratory
depression or cardiovascular complications; because flumazenil is
short-acting and diazepam's effects can last for days, several doses may
be necessary. Overdoses of diazepam combined with alcohol, opiates, or
other depressants may be fatal. 


# Cyclobenzaprine

## Overview

Cyclobenzaprine, sold under brand names including Flexeril, is a muscle
relaxer used for muscle spasms from musculoskeletal conditions of sudden
onset. It decreases pain in the first two weeks, peaking in the first few
days, but has no proven benefit after two weeks, so therapy should not be
continued long-term. Chemically, it is very similar to tricyclic
antidepressants like amitriptyline, and it inhibits serotonin and
norepinephrine reuptake while blocking serotonin, adrenergic, histamine,
and muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. 

## Mechanism

Cyclobenzaprine differs from amitriptyline in structure only by the
presence of a single double bond within the tricyclic ring system 
it is chemically 10,11-dehydroamitriptyline. Its known actions include
serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibition, serotonin receptor
antagonism (5-HT2A, 5-HT2B, 5-HT2C, 5-HT6, 5-HT7), alpha-1 and alpha-2
adrenergic receptor antagonism, histamine H1 receptor antagonism, and
muscarinic acetylcholine receptor antagonism (M1, M2, M3). The
antihistamine activity of cyclobenzaprine is thought to play a major
role in its sedative effects. Its mechanism of action as a muscle
relaxant itself is unknown, but may work through modulating serotonergic
and noradrenergic systems rather than acting directly on muscle tissue.


## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Cyclobenzaprine results in increased rates of drowsiness (38%), dry
mouth (24%), and dizziness (10%). Drowsiness and dry mouth appear to
intensify with increasing dose.

Agitation is a common side effect observed, especially in the elderly.
Some experts believe that cyclobenzaprine should be avoided in elderly
patients because it can cause confusion, delirium, and cognitive
impairment. Dysphagia, a life-threatening side effect, may rarely occur;
treatment protocols and support should follow the same approach as for
structurally related tricyclics such as tricyclic antidepressants.


## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Cyclobenzaprine has an oral bioavailability of about 33-55% and
approximately 93% is bound to plasma proteins. Its active metabolite is
norcyclobenzaprine. The elimination half-life is 18 hours for the
immediate-release formulation (range 8-37 hours) and 32 hours for the
extended-release formulation. Unlike diazepam or diphenhydramine,
specific elderly-versus-young pharmacokinetic comparison data is not
well documented in general references for this drug  the elevated
elderly risk is established clinically and through guideline
recommendations rather than through published elderly-specific half-life
studies. 

## Dosing Considerations

The National Committee for Quality Assurance recommends avoiding the use
of cyclobenzaprine in the elderly entirely because of the potential for
more severe side effects, and it is listed among potentially
inappropriate medications for the elderly under the Beers criteria.
Since no benefit is proven beyond two weeks of use in any age group,
treatment duration should be strictly limited regardless of patient age.


## Interactions

Cyclobenzaprine has major contraindications with monoamine oxidase
inhibitors (MAOIs)  use within two weeks of an MAOI may result in
serious, life-threatening side effects. Increased risk of serotonin
syndrome has been found when cyclobenzaprine is taken with serotonergic
drugs such as duloxetine or phenelzine. Central nervous system
depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, major
tranquilizers) may also interact with cyclobenzaprine, compounding
sedation. Given cyclobenzaprine's own antihistamine and anticholinergic
activity, combination with other anticholinergic drugs such as
diphenhydramine or amitriptyline compounds anticholinergic burden rather
than producing two independent risks. 

## Monitoring

Given the dysphagia risk and cognitive effects flagged specifically for
elderly patients, monitoring should include swallowing function,
mental status (confusion, delirium), and cardiac rhythm, in addition to
the standard sedation and dry mouth watch. Any patient on both
cyclobenzaprine and a serotonergic antidepressant should be monitored
for serotonin syndrome symptoms.

## Alternatives

Cyclobenzaprine has been found just as effective as tizanidine,
orphenadrine, and carisoprodol for acute lower back pain, with no
proven advantage of any one agent, so an agent with a more favorable
elderly safety profile may be substituted without expected loss of
efficacy. Given the guideline-level recommendation to avoid this drug
class in the elderly altogether, non-pharmacologic approaches (physical
therapy, heat/ice) should be considered first-line where feasible.

## Urgent Signs

The most common effects of overdose are drowsiness and tachycardia.
Rare but potentially critical complications are cardiac arrest,
abnormal heart rhythms, severe low blood pressure, seizures, and
neuroleptic malignant syndrome. The potential for harm is increased
when central nervous system depressants and antidepressants are also
used. 


# Ibuprofen

## Overview

Ibuprofen is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) used to
relieve pain, fever, and inflammation, including painful menstrual
periods, migraines, and rheumatoid arthritis. It works by inhibiting the
production of prostaglandins by decreasing the activity of the enzyme
cyclooxygenase (COX). Unlike the other drug classes in this knowledge
base, ibuprofen's primary geriatric ADR risk is not anticholinergic or
sedative  it is gastrointestinal bleeding and renal/cardiovascular
risk, driven by a distinct mechanism. 

## Mechanism

Ibuprofen inhibits cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, which convert
arachidonic acid to prostaglandin H2 (PGH2). PGH2 is converted into
prostaglandins, which mediate pain, inflammation, and fever, and into
thromboxane A2, which stimulates platelet aggregation and promotes blood
clot formation. Ibuprofen is a nonselective COX inhibitor, inhibiting
both COX-1 and COX-2. Inhibition of COX-1 is believed to be responsible
for unwanted gastrointestinal effects, since COX-1 has a protective role
in the stomach lining; this is the direct mechanistic link to
ibuprofen's bleeding and ulceration risk. 

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Common side effects include heartburn, nausea, indigestion, and
abdominal pain. Adverse effects include gastrointestinal ulceration,
salt and fluid retention, and high blood pressure. Infrequent adverse
effects include esophageal ulceration, heart failure, high blood levels
of potassium, kidney impairment, confusion, and bronchospasm. Long-term
use has been associated with kidney failure and, rarely, liver failure,
and it can exacerbate heart failure. Along with several other NSAIDs,
chronic ibuprofen use is correlated with progression to hypertension and
myocardial infarction, particularly among those chronically using higher
doses; at low doses, it does not appear to increase heart attack risk,
but at higher doses it may. Ibuprofen can also worsen asthma, sometimes
fatally. Skin reactions including bullous pemphigoid-like blistering and,
rarely, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, have been reported. 

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Ibuprofen has an elimination half-life of only 2-4 hours, considerably
shorter than the other drug classes in this knowledge base, and no
elderly-specific half-life extension is documented in general
references the way it is for diazepam or diphenhydramine. However,
elderly patients are disproportionately affected by ibuprofen's renal
and cardiovascular risks rather than its pharmacokinetics per se: age
-related decline in baseline renal function means the same dose carries
a higher risk of precipitating kidney impairment or failure in an older
patient, and pre-existing hypertension or heart failure (more prevalent
in geriatric populations) is more likely to be exacerbated. 

## Dosing Considerations

Toxic effects from overdose are unlikely at doses below 100 mg/kg, but
can be severe above 400 mg/kg. For chronic use, cardiovascular and renal
risk correlates with dose level and duration, meaning the lowest
effective dose for the shortest necessary duration is the relevant
safety principle in older adults, rather than a simple age-based dose
reduction as with sedative/anticholinergic drug classes. 

## Interactions

**Aspirin (critical for antiplatelet combination patients):** Ibuprofen
can interfere with the antiplatelet effect of low-dose aspirin,
potentially rendering aspirin less effective when used for
cardioprotection and stroke prevention. Sufficient time should be
allowed between doses: 30 minutes or more for ibuprofen taken after
immediate-release aspirin, and 8 hours or more for ibuprofen taken
before immediate-release aspirin. This timing guidance does not apply to
enteric-coated aspirin. If ibuprofen is taken only occasionally without
following the recommended timing, the reduction in aspirin's
cardioprotective effect is minimal, but regular concurrent use without
proper timing is a genuine interaction concern.

**Alcohol:** Drinking alcohol while taking ibuprofen may increase the
risk of stomach bleeding  a directly compounding risk on top of
ibuprofen's own GI bleeding potential.

**Other anticoagulants/antiplatelets:** Combination with any
anticoagulant or antiplatelet agent (not just aspirin) compounds
bleeding risk through the combination of impaired platelet function
(from the NSAID) and reduced clotting ability (from the anticoagulant),
which is the core mechanism behind the severe/bleeding-risk tier of ADR
in this knowledge base. 

## Monitoring

Given the renal, cardiovascular, and GI risks, monitoring for
long-term or high-dose ibuprofen use in older adults should include
kidney function, blood pressure, and signs of GI bleeding (dark stools,
abdominal pain, unexplained anemia). Any patient on both ibuprofen and
an antiplatelet or anticoagulant agent warrants closer bleeding-risk
monitoring than either drug alone would require.

## Alternatives

About 60% of people respond to any NSAID, so switching to a different
NSAID does not necessarily avoid the class-level bleeding and renal
risks. For pain or fever management in older adults, paracetamol
(acetaminophen) is generally considered a safer alternative where
appropriate, since it lacks the antiplatelet and COX-mediated GI risks
of NSAIDs, though it carries its own hepatotoxicity considerations at
high doses.

## Urgent Signs

Most overdose symptoms are an excess of ibuprofen's pharmacological
action and include abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, drowsiness,
dizziness, headache, and ear ringing. Rarely, more severe symptoms such
as gastrointestinal bleeding, seizures, metabolic acidosis, high
potassium levels, low blood pressure, abnormal heart rhythms, coma,
liver dysfunction, acute kidney failure, and cardiac arrest have been
reported. Any signs of active GI bleeding (blood in stool or vomit,
unexplained severe abdominal pain) in a patient on ibuprofen 
especially one also on an antiplatelet or anticoagulant  warrant
immediate evaluation rather than routine follow-up. 

# Aspirin

## Overview

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug
(NSAID) used at higher doses for pain, fever, and inflammation, and at
low doses (typically 81 mg/day) for antiplatelet cardioprotection and
stroke prevention. In geriatric patients it most commonly appears as
low-dose "baby aspirin" for cardiovascular prophylaxis rather than as an
analgesic, which shapes its interaction risk profile in this knowledge
base -- it is included here specifically for the severe/bleeding-risk
tier alongside ibuprofen, since the two combined compound antiplatelet
and gastrointestinal bleeding risk beyond either drug alone.

## Mechanism

Aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2) by
acetylating a serine residue in the enzyme's active site, which
permanently blocks that platelet's ability to produce thromboxane A2 for
the remainder of its roughly 7-10 day lifespan. This irreversibility is
the key mechanistic difference from ibuprofen, which reversibly and
competitively inhibits COX -- aspirin's antiplatelet effect persists
well after the drug itself has been cleared, while ibuprofen's does not.
This same irreversible COX-1 inhibition in the gastric mucosa is what
drives aspirin's GI bleeding risk.

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

The most clinically significant ADR at low antiplatelet doses is
increased bleeding risk, particularly gastrointestinal bleeding and
hemorrhagic stroke. At higher (analgesic/anti-inflammatory) doses,
aspirin shares ibuprofen's broader NSAID profile: dyspepsia, nausea,
gastric ulceration, and renal impairment. Tinnitus and hearing
impairment can occur at high doses (salicylate toxicity) but are
uncommon at cardioprotective doses. Aspirin can also precipitate
bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive asthmatics and, rarely, hypersensitivity
reactions ranging from urticaria to anaphylaxis.

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Aspirin's elimination half-life is dose-dependent: roughly 2-3 hours at
low antiplatelet doses but extending to 15-30 hours at high
anti-inflammatory doses due to saturation of hepatic metabolism. Because
the antiplatelet effect is driven by irreversible platelet inhibition
rather than ongoing drug presence, the short plasma half-life at low
dose does not reflect the duration of clinical effect. Older adults have
reduced renal clearance of salicylate metabolites, which can prolong
elevated levels at higher doses and increase toxicity risk, though this
is less relevant at standard 81 mg cardioprotective dosing.

## Dosing Considerations

For cardioprotection, standard dosing is 81 mg once daily; higher doses
do not improve antiplatelet efficacy and increase bleeding risk without
added benefit. For pain or fever, geriatric patients should use the
lowest effective dose given the age-related increase in GI bleeding and
renal risk shared with other NSAIDs. Enteric-coated formulations are
often used to reduce direct gastric irritation, though this does not
eliminate systemic bleeding risk from platelet inhibition.

## Interactions

**Ibuprofen (critical for this knowledge base's combination case):**
Ibuprofen competes with aspirin for the same COX-1 binding site. If
ibuprofen is taken before aspirin, or too soon after, it can physically
block aspirin from acetylating COX-1, blunting or negating aspirin's
antiplatelet effect. Beyond this pharmacodynamic interference, taking
the two together also produces additive GI bleeding risk, since both
drugs independently impair the stomach's protective prostaglandin
function. Timing separation (ibuprofen at least 30 minutes after or 8
hours before immediate-release aspirin) can preserve aspirin's
cardioprotective effect, but does not reduce the combined bleeding risk.

**Other anticoagulants/antiplatelets:** Combining aspirin with warfarin,
DOACs (e.g., apixaban), or other antiplatelet agents (e.g., clopidogrel)
substantially increases major bleeding risk and requires close clinical
judgment about whether combination therapy is warranted.

**Alcohol:** Increases the risk of GI bleeding when combined with
aspirin, compounding aspirin's own gastric mucosal effects.

## Monitoring

Patients on chronic aspirin, especially in combination with another
NSAID or anticoagulant, should be monitored for signs of GI bleeding
(dark or tarry stools, unexplained anemia, abdominal pain) and for renal
function periodically. Patients on dual antiplatelet or
antiplatelet-plus-anticoagulant regimens warrant closer bleeding
surveillance than aspirin monotherapy.

## Alternatives

For cardioprotection, clopidogrel is sometimes used as an alternative in
patients who cannot tolerate aspirin, though it carries its own bleeding
risk profile and is not simply a "safer" substitute. For pain or fever
where aspirin is being used off-label for analgesia rather than
cardioprotection, acetaminophen is generally preferred in older adults
given its lack of antiplatelet and COX-mediated GI effects.

## Urgent Signs

Salicylate toxicity (from overdose or accumulation at high doses)
presents with tinnitus, hyperventilation, nausea, vomiting, and in
severe cases metabolic acidosis, altered mental status, seizures, and
coma. In the context of standard low-dose cardioprotective use, the more
relevant urgent presentation is active GI bleeding (hematemesis,
melena, unexplained hypotension) or signs of hemorrhagic stroke (sudden
severe headache, neurological deficits) either warrants immediate
emergency evaluation, especially in a patient also taking ibuprofen or
another antiplatelet/anticoagulant.

# Oxybutynin

## Overview

Oxybutynin is an antimuscarinic drug used for overactive bladder symptoms:
urgency, frequency, leakage, urge incontinence and dysuria in patients with
uninhibited or reflex neurogenic bladder. In geriatric patients it often
shows up for urinary incontinence, which makes it a common source of
anticholinergic burden in older adults.  

## Mechanism

Oxybutynin is an antimuscarinic with direct antispasmodic action on
bladder smooth muscle. It increases bladder capacity, reduces uninhibited
detrusor contractions, and delays the first urge to void. Most of the
antimuscarinic activity comes from the R-isomer, and its metabolite
desethyloxybutynin is also active. It crosses into the CNS, so the
same antimuscarinic action that relaxes the bladder can cause confusion
and cognitive effects.  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Most adverse effects are anticholinergic. Dry mouth is the most common and
is dose-related. Other reported effects include somnolence, confusion,
constipation, blurred vision and flushing. CNS anticholinergic effects
such as hallucinations, agitation, confusion and somnolence have been
reported, mostly in the first months of therapy or after a dose increase.
Postmarketing reports include angioedema with airway obstruction,
convulsions, tachycardia, QT prolongation and anaphylaxis. Because
oxybutynin reduces sweating, it can cause heat prostration (fever, heat
stroke) in high environmental temperatures.  

Bladder antimuscarinics are one of the anticholinergic classes most
strongly associated with dementia risk in large observational studies,
unlike antihistamines. 

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

In frail elderly patients the elimination half-life is prolonged from
about 2-3 hours to about 5 hours. Oxybutynin is metabolized mainly by
CYP3A4, has very low oral bioavailability (about 6%), and is more than 99%
protein bound.  

## Dosing Considerations

The usual adult dose is 5 mg two to three times daily, with a maximum of
5 mg four times daily. For frail elderly patients, the recommended
starting dose is lower: 2.5 mg two or three times daily. No specific
renal or hepatic dose adjustment is given; use with caution.  

## Interactions

Other anticholinergic drugs, or drugs that cause dry mouth, constipation
or drowsiness, add to oxybutynin's effects. A patient on oxybutynin plus
diphenhydramine, amitriptyline or cyclobenzaprine faces compounded
anticholinergic burden rather than separate risks. Ketoconazole, a strong
CYP3A4 inhibitor, raised oxybutynin levels about 3-4 fold; other CYP3A4
inhibitors such as itraconazole, erythromycin and clarithromycin may also
raise levels. Oxybutynin may block the prokinetic effect of metoclopramide.
Alcohol increases drowsiness. Use with caution in patients with dementia on
cholinesterase inhibitors, since the two drugs work in opposite directions,
and in Parkinson's disease.  

## Monitoring

Monitor for CNS anticholinergic effects (confusion, agitation,
hallucinations, somnolence), especially in the first months and after a
dose increase, and reduce or stop the dose if they appear. Also check for
constipation, urinary retention and heat intolerance in hot weather.
Cognitive status should be checked at each visit in elderly patients,
especially those with dementia.  

## Alternatives

For overactive bladder in older adults, non-drug approaches such as
bladder training and timed voiding are first-line. If a drug is needed,
mirabegron (a beta-3 agonist without anticholinergic burden) or an
antimuscarinic with less CNS penetration, such as trospium, is generally
preferred over immediate-release oxybutynin. 

## Urgent Signs

Overdose causes CNS excitation (restlessness, tremor, irritability,
convulsions, delirium, hallucinations), flushing, fever, dehydration,
arrhythmia, vomiting and urinary retention. Severe cases can progress to
hypotension or hypertension, respiratory failure, paralysis and coma.
Swelling of the face, tongue or throat suggests angioedema, which can be
life-threatening and needs immediate evaluation. Treatment is symptomatic
and supportive.  


# Zolpidem

## Overview

Zolpidem is a non-benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotic (a "Z-drug") used for
short-term treatment of insomnia with difficulty falling asleep. It is a
Schedule IV controlled substance. Although it is chemically different from
benzodiazepines, it acts on the same receptor and carries similar
fall and cognitive risks in older adults.  

## Mechanism

Zolpidem modulates the GABA-A receptor, like benzodiazepines, but binds
preferentially to the omega-1 (alpha-1 subunit) receptor subtype. This
subtype mediates sedation, which is why zolpidem is mainly hypnotic, with
less anxiolytic and muscle-relaxant effect than benzodiazepines.  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Common effects include drowsiness, dizziness, a "drugged" feeling,
headache, diarrhea and dry mouth. In longer trials (28-35 nights),
dizziness occurred in 5% vs 1% on placebo and drowsiness in 8% vs 5%.
Anterograde amnesia has been reported, mainly at doses above 10 mg.
Next-day impairment is possible, and patients are advised not to drive or
operate machinery the day after dosing if they are not fully alert.
 

Complex sleep behaviors have been reported: sleep-driving, preparing food,
making phone calls and other activities while not fully awake, often
with no memory of the event. Risk is higher with alcohol, other CNS
depressants or doses above the maximum.  

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Compared with adults aged 20-40, patients over 70 had about 50% higher
peak concentration, 32% longer half-life and 64% higher total exposure
(AUC) after a single dose. In non-US trials, falls occurred in 1.5% of
patients, and 93% of those who fell were 70 or older.  

## Dosing Considerations

The recommended dose for elderly or debilitated patients is 5 mg
immediately before bedtime, half the older standard adult dose of 10 mg.
Patients with hepatic impairment should also start at 5 mg, since
cirrhotic patients had about 5 times higher exposure and a half-life of
9.9 hours vs 2.2 hours. The maximum dose is 10 mg per day.  

The linked label is from 2007. Current FDA labeling recommends 5 mg as
the starting dose for women and adds a boxed warning for complex sleep
behaviors. 

## Interactions

Other CNS depressants (benzodiazepines such as diazepam, opioids such as
tramadol, sedating antihistamines, gabapentin) add to sedation and may
require dose reduction. Alcohol should not be combined with zolpidem.
Sertraline raised zolpidem peak levels by 43%. Imipramine and
chlorpromazine add to decreased alertness. Itraconazole raised exposure
by 34%, and rifampin reduced it by 73%.  

## Monitoring

Monitor for next-day sedation, falls, confusion, memory problems and any
complex sleep behavior. Zolpidem should be stopped if a patient has a
complex sleep behavior episode. Reassess the need for ongoing use, since
it is intended for short-term treatment.  

## Alternatives

For insomnia in older adults, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia
(CBT-I) and sleep hygiene are first-line. If a drug is needed, low-dose
doxepin (3-6 mg) or ramelteon carry less fall and cognitive risk than
Z-drugs or benzodiazepines. 

## Urgent Signs

Overdose ranges from somnolence to coma. Overdoses of zolpidem alone up
to 400 mg (40 times the maximum dose) recovered fully, but overdoses
combined with other CNS depressants were more severe, including fatal
cases. Treatment is supportive. Flumazenil may reverse the effect, and
zolpidem is not removed by dialysis. A fall with head injury in an
elderly patient on zolpidem needs urgent evaluation.  


# Tramadol

## Overview

Tramadol is a centrally acting synthetic opioid used for moderate to
moderately severe pain in adults. It is often seen as a "weaker" opioid in
geriatric patients, but its serotonergic activity and seizure risk give it
interaction risks that other opioids do not carry.  

## Mechanism

Tramadol works through two mechanisms. The parent drug binds weakly to
mu-opioid receptors, while its O-demethylated metabolite M1 binds with much
higher affinity and is up to 200 times more potent at the mu receptor. The
parent drug also weakly inhibits serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake.
M1 is formed by CYP2D6, so analgesic effect and toxicity depend on CYP2D6
activity. About 7% of people are poor metabolizers, with about 40% lower M1
levels.  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Over up to 90 days, common effects were constipation (46%), nausea (40%),
dizziness/vertigo (33%), headache (32%), somnolence (25%), vomiting
(17%) and pruritus (11%). Serious reactions include seizures (even within
the recommended dose range), serotonin syndrome, respiratory depression,
anaphylactoid reactions (often after the first dose), and withdrawal on
abrupt stopping.  

Newer tramadol labeling also warns about hyponatremia (SIADH) and
hypoglycemia, both relevant to older adults and to patients on SSRIs or
sulfonylureas. 

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

In patients over 75, peak serum concentrations were higher (208 vs 162
ng/mL in patients aged 65-75) and the elimination half-life was longer (7
vs 6 hours). Treatment-limiting GI adverse events occurred in 30% of
patients over 75, compared with 17% of those under 65.  

## Dosing Considerations

The adult dose is titrated by 50 mg/day every 3 days up to 200 mg/day,
then 50-100 mg every 4-6 hours as needed, with a maximum of 400 mg/day.
For patients over 75, the maximum is 300 mg/day, and older adults should
start at the low end of the range. With CrCl below 30 mL/min, the
interval is extended to every 12 hours with a maximum of 200 mg/day. In
cirrhosis, the dose is 50 mg every 12 hours.  

## Interactions

Serotonergic drugs (SSRIs such as sertraline, SNRIs, TCAs such as
amitriptyline, MAOIs, triptans, linezolid, lithium) raise the risk of
serotonin syndrome. SSRIs, TCAs, MAOIs and neuroleptics also lower the
seizure threshold. CYP2D6 inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine, amitriptyline,
and sertraline to a lesser degree) raise tramadol levels and lower M1,
increasing seizure and serotonin syndrome risk. CYP3A4 inhibitors reduce
tramadol clearance. Carbamazepine increases tramadol metabolism and is not
recommended with it. Other CNS depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines such as
diazepam, sedative-hypnotics such as zolpidem, gabapentin) add CNS and
respiratory depression. Altered warfarin effect, including raised
prothrombin time, has been reported rarely.  

## Monitoring

Monitor for sedation, respiratory depression, constipation and falls,
especially when starting therapy or with other CNS depressants. In any
patient also on a serotonergic drug, watch for serotonin syndrome
(agitation, tremor, fever, sweating, diarrhea, muscle rigidity). Check
sodium if confusion develops in a patient also on an SSRI or diuretic, and
check INR in patients on warfarin.   

## Alternatives

For mild to moderate pain in older adults, scheduled acetaminophen is
usually first-line. Topical agents (e.g. topical diclofenac or lidocaine)
avoid most systemic risk for localized pain. If an opioid is needed in a
patient on serotonergic drugs, a non-serotonergic opioid at a low dose
avoids the serotonin syndrome and seizure risk tramadol adds. 

## Urgent Signs

Overdose causes respiratory depression, somnolence progressing to coma,
seizures, bradycardia, hypotension and death. Naloxone reverses some but
not all effects and may increase seizure risk. Seizures, or signs of
serotonin syndrome (high fever, severe rigidity, confusion), especially in
a patient also on sertraline or amitriptyline, need immediate emergency
evaluation.  


# Glyburide

## Overview

Glyburide (glibenclamide) is a sulfonylurea used with diet and exercise
to improve glycemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes. It is one of
the most common causes of medication-induced hypoglycemia, with higher
risk than other sulfonylureas, which makes it a high-priority drug in
geriatric patients.   

## Mechanism

Glyburide binds and inhibits the SUR1 subunit of ATP-sensitive potassium
channels in pancreatic beta cells. This depolarizes the cell, raises
intracellular calcium and triggers insulin release, independently of the
current blood glucose. This glucose-independent insulin release is what
causes hypoglycemia when the patient eats less or kidney function drops.
  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Hypoglycemia is the most important adverse effect. Other effects include
nausea, epigastric fullness and heartburn (about 1.8%), allergic skin
reactions (about 1.5%), weight gain, hyponatremia, blurred vision, and
rarely cholestatic jaundice, hepatitis, blood disorders and disulfiram-like
reactions with alcohol. Glyburide can cause hemolytic anemia in G6PD
deficiency.   

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Glyburide is metabolized in the liver (mainly CYP2C9) to weakly active
metabolites and excreted about half in bile and half in urine, with a
terminal half-life of about 10 hours. Elderly patients are particularly
prone to hypoglycemia, and age-related decline in renal function lets
glyburide and its active metabolites accumulate, extending hypoglycemia
risk. Hypoglycemia can also be harder to recognize in older adults.
  

## Dosing Considerations

The usual starting dose is 2.5-5 mg once daily with breakfast, or 1.25 mg
daily in more sensitive patients. Doses are increased by no more than
2.5 mg weekly, with a maintenance range of 1.25-20 mg daily and no more
than 20 mg daily. Elderly, debilitated or malnourished patients, and those
with renal or hepatic impairment, need conservative starting and
maintenance doses, and renal function should be assessed.  

## Interactions

Drugs that increase the hypoglycemic effect include NSAIDs (ibuprofen),
salicylates (aspirin at higher doses), sulfonamides, warfarin and other
coumarins, MAO inhibitors, probenecid, ciprofloxacin and oral miconazole.
Beta-blockers can both increase hypoglycemia and mask its symptoms.
Thiazides, corticosteroids, estrogens and phenytoin can raise glucose and
reduce control. Colesevelam reduces absorption, so glyburide should be
taken at least 4 hours before it. Bosentan is contraindicated. Alcohol
increases hypoglycemia risk and can cause a disulfiram-like reaction.
Tramadol may also contribute to hypoglycemia.   

## Monitoring

Monitor blood glucose, especially when starting therapy, changing the
dose, adding an interacting drug, or when intake drops (illness, poor
appetite). Check renal function at baseline and periodically. Check HbA1c
to confirm the target is appropriate for the patient's age and frailty,
since overly tight control raises hypoglycemia risk in older adults.
  

## Alternatives

In older adults, glipizide (shorter-acting, no active renal metabolites) is
preferred if a sulfonylurea is needed. Metformin (if renal function allows),
DPP-4 inhibitors, or SGLT2 inhibitors carry much lower hypoglycemia risk.


## Urgent Signs

Severe hypoglycemia presents with confusion, sweating, tremor, unusual
behavior, seizures or coma, and in an older adult may look like a stroke
or delirium. Coma or seizures are a medical emergency: IV 50% glucose
followed by a 10% glucose infusion to keep blood glucose above 100 mg/dL.
Patients need monitoring for at least 24-48 hours because hypoglycemia can
recur.   Octreotide is often used for recurrent
sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia. 


# Omeprazole

## Overview

Omeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) used for GERD, erosive
esophagitis, duodenal and gastric ulcers, H. pylori eradication and
hypersecretory conditions. It is very commonly used long-term in older
adults, often without a clear ongoing indication, and its main geriatric
risks come from long-term use and CYP2C19 interactions.  

## Mechanism

Omeprazole inhibits the H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump) in gastric parietal
cells, blocking the final step of acid production. The effect starts
within an hour, peaks within 2 hours and lasts up to 72 hours; acid
secretion returns to baseline over 3-5 days after stopping. It is
metabolized mainly by CYP2C19 and CYP3A4, and also inhibits CYP2C19, which
explains most of its interactions.  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Common short-term effects are headache (7%), abdominal pain (5%), nausea
(4%), diarrhea (4%), vomiting (3%) and flatulence (3%). Long-term use
carries additional risks: hip, wrist and spine fractures with high-dose or
long-term (1 year or more) use; C. difficile-associated diarrhea;
hypomagnesemia (usually after 1 year); vitamin B12 deficiency (after 3
years); acute tubulointerstitial nephritis; cutaneous and systemic lupus;
and fundic gland polyps.  

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

In elderly patients, plasma clearance is about half that of young adults
(about 250 mL/min), the half-life is about twice as long (about 1 hour),
and bioavailability is higher (76% vs 58%). No dose adjustment is required
for age alone. Patients of Asian descent have about 4 times higher
exposure because of more frequent CYP2C19 poor metabolism.  

## Dosing Considerations

Typical doses are 20 mg daily for GERD and duodenal ulcer, and 40 mg daily
for gastric ulcer. For erosive esophagitis maintenance in hepatic
impairment or Asian patients, 10 mg daily is recommended. Use the lowest
dose and shortest duration that fits the condition, to reduce C. difficile,
fracture and other long-term risks.  

## Interactions

Omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19, raising levels of diazepam (more sedation)
and amitriptyline (increased risk of delirium). It reduces activation of
clopidogrel and should not be combined with it. It can increase INR in
patients on warfarin, and may raise levels of high-dose methotrexate,
phenytoin and digoxin. Citalopram should be capped at 20 mg/day with
omeprazole. Because it reduces gastric acid, it can lower absorption of
iron salts, ketoconazole/itraconazole, mycophenolate and some antiretrovirals
(atazanavir, rilpivirine). St. John's wort and rifampin lower omeprazole
levels and should be avoided.  

## Monitoring

Reassess the indication regularly and deprescribe if there is no ongoing
need. For long-term use, consider periodic magnesium levels (especially with
diuretics or digoxin), B12 levels, renal function and bone health. Monitor
INR in patients on warfarin and sedation in patients on diazepam.
 

## Alternatives

If acid suppression is still needed but long-term PPI risk is a concern,
step down to the lowest effective PPI dose or on-demand use, or switch to
an H2 blocker such as famotidine (dose-adjusted for renal function). If
the PPI is only for clopidogrel-related GI protection, pantoprazole has
less CYP2C19 interaction. 

## Urgent Signs

Overdose is generally mild: confusion, drowsiness, blurred vision,
tachycardia, nausea and vomiting have been reported and were transient,
with doses up to 2,400 mg reported. More relevant urgent presentations in
older adults are severe or bloody diarrhea (possible C. difficile), signs
of severe hypomagnesemia (tetany, arrhythmia, seizures), or a sudden drop
in kidney function (possible interstitial nephritis).  


# Warfarin

## Overview

Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist anticoagulant used for atrial
fibrillation, venous thromboembolism and mechanical heart valves. It has a
narrow therapeutic range, many drug and food interactions, and a boxed
warning for major or fatal bleeding, making it one of the drugs most often
linked to ADR-related hospitalizations in older adults.  

## Mechanism

Warfarin inhibits VKORC1 (vitamin K epoxide reductase), reducing
regeneration of vitamin K. This lowers active vitamin K-dependent clotting
factors II, VII, IX and X, and the anticoagulant proteins C and S. The
anticoagulant effect starts within 24 hours, but the peak takes 72-96
hours, and a single dose lasts 2-5 days.  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Bleeding is the main adverse effect and can occur in any tissue or organ.
Risk is highest at the start of therapy and with high INR. Risk factors
include INR above 4.0, age 65 or older, highly variable INR, prior GI
bleeding, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, anemia, malignancy, renal
insufficiency and interacting drugs. Less common effects include skin and
tissue necrosis (under 0.1%), purple toes syndrome and cholesterol
microembolization.  

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Patients 60 and older have a greater-than-expected INR response to a given
dose. Warfarin is about 99% protein bound and metabolized mainly by
CYP2C9, with an effective half-life of 20-60 hours (mean about 40 hours).
CYP2C9 and VKORC1 variants lower dose requirements; carriers of CYP2C9*3
needed on average 37% lower doses and have higher bleeding risk.  

## Dosing Considerations

Warfarin is usually started at 2-5 mg/day and adjusted by INR. Elderly,
debilitated or genetically sensitive patients need lower starting and
maintenance doses, and large loading doses should be avoided. Typical
maintenance is 2-10 mg daily. For most indications the INR target is 2.0-3.0
(2.5 for VTE). INR above 4.0 gives no extra benefit and increases bleeding.
 

## Interactions

Drugs that increase INR or bleeding risk include NSAIDs (ibuprofen),
aspirin and other antiplatelets, SSRIs (sertraline), many antibiotics
(metronidazole, fluoroquinolones, erythromycin, trimethoprim-
sulfamethoxazole), amiodarone, fluconazole and acetaminophen at regular
doses. Omeprazole and tramadol can also raise INR. Drugs that lower INR
include carbamazepine, rifampin, barbiturates and vitamin K. Herbal
products such as ginkgo, garlic, dong quai and cranberry can increase the
effect; St. John's wort and coenzyme Q10 can decrease it. Diet should keep
vitamin K intake consistent, avoiding big changes in leafy greens.
 

## Monitoring

Check INR daily after the first dose until stable, then every 1-4 weeks.
Check more often whenever an interacting drug is started or stopped.
Monitor for signs of bleeding (bruising, dark stools, blood in urine,
nosebleeds) and hemoglobin. Fall risk matters especially in elderly
patients on warfarin, because a fall can lead to intracranial bleeding.
  

## Alternatives

For non-valvular atrial fibrillation and VTE, direct oral anticoagulants
such as apixaban are generally preferred over warfarin in older adults
because they have lower intracranial bleeding rates and fewer
interactions. Warfarin remains the choice for mechanical heart valves.
  

## Urgent Signs

Signs of major bleeding need immediate evaluation: vomiting blood, black or
tarry stools, blood in urine, severe headache, sudden neurological
changes (possible intracranial bleed), or a fall with head injury. For high
INR or bleeding, warfarin is stopped and vitamin K given (5-25 mg IV for
major bleeding). Severe hemorrhage may need fresh frozen plasma or factor
concentrates.   Four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate
(4F-PCC) is now the standard rapid reversal for major bleeding.



# Apixaban

## Overview

Apixaban (Eliquis) is a direct oral factor Xa inhibitor used to reduce
stroke risk in non-valvular atrial fibrillation, to prevent DVT/PE after
hip or knee replacement, and to treat DVT/PE. It is increasingly common in
geriatric patients as a replacement for warfarin, and its main ADR risk is
bleeding, especially when combined with antiplatelets, NSAIDs or SSRIs.
 

## Mechanism

Apixaban selectively inhibits both free and clot-bound factor Xa and
prothrombinase activity, without needing antithrombin III. This reduces
thrombin generation and clot formation. It has no direct effect on
platelets but indirectly reduces thrombin-induced platelet aggregation.
 

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Bleeding is the most common adverse reaction. In the ARISTOTLE trial
(atrial fibrillation, vs warfarin, per 100 patient-years), major bleeding
was 2.13 vs 3.09, intracranial bleeding 0.33 vs 0.82, and fatal bleeding
0.06 vs 0.24. GI bleeding was similar (0.83 vs 0.93). Boxed warnings cover
increased clotting risk if stopped early, and spinal/epidural hematoma
with neuraxial anesthesia.  

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Over 69% of patients in the atrial fibrillation trials were 65 or older,
and over 31% were 75 or older, with no meaningful differences in safety or
effectiveness by age. The half-life is about 12 hours. About 27% of
clearance is renal, and it is metabolized mainly by CYP3A4 and is a P-gp
substrate. Age, low body weight and reduced renal function raise exposure,
which is why they drive the dose reduction.  

## Dosing Considerations

For atrial fibrillation, the standard dose is 5 mg twice daily. The dose
is reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily if the patient has at least 2 of: age 80
or older, body weight 60 kg or less, serum creatinine 1.5 mg/dL or higher.
For DVT/PE treatment it is 10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice
daily. It is not recommended in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C).
 

## Interactions

Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel), chronic NSAIDs (ibuprofen), SSRIs
(sertraline), SNRIs and heparin all increase bleeding risk. In ARISTOTLE,
adding aspirin nearly doubled the bleeding rate (1.8% to 3.4% per year).
Combined strong CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors (ketoconazole, itraconazole,
ritonavir) need a 50% dose reduction, or avoidance if the patient is
already on 2.5 mg twice daily. Combined strong inducers (rifampin,
carbamazepine, phenytoin, St. John's wort) lower apixaban levels and should
be avoided.  

## Monitoring

Check renal function, weight and age at least yearly to confirm the dose
still matches the reduction criteria. Monitor for bleeding signs and
hemoglobin, especially in patients also on aspirin, NSAIDs or SSRIs. Check
adherence, since missed doses quickly reduce protection with a 12-hour
half-life. Routine coagulation monitoring is not required.   

## Alternatives

Apixaban is often already the preferred anticoagulant in older adults.
When bleeding risk is high, review whether concomitant antiplatelets or
NSAIDs are necessary before considering a change of anticoagulant. Warfarin
is used instead for mechanical valves or when cost or severe renal disease
rules out apixaban. 

## Urgent Signs

Major bleeding needs immediate evaluation: GI bleeding, blood in urine,
severe headache or sudden neurological deficits, or a fall with head
injury. The anticoagulant effect lasts at least 24 hours after the last
dose. Andexanet alfa specifically reverses apixaban; PCC may be considered
but has not been studied in trials. Vitamin K and protamine do not reverse
it. Activated charcoal reduces absorption in overdose if given early.
 


# Sertraline

## Overview

Sertraline is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) approved for
major depressive disorder and premenstrual dysphoric disorder, and widely
used for anxiety disorders. It is often the preferred antidepressant in
older adults, but it carries geriatric-specific risks of hyponatremia,
bleeding and falls.  

## Mechanism

Sertraline inhibits serotonin reuptake in CNS neurons, with very weak
effects on norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake. It has minimal
anticholinergic activity, which is the main reason it is preferred over
TCAs like amitriptyline in older adults. Serotonin reuptake inhibition in
platelets reduces platelet serotonin, which explains its bleeding risk.
  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Common adverse effects vs placebo include nausea (26% vs 12%), diarrhea
(18% vs 9%), insomnia (16% vs 9%), dry mouth (16% vs 9%), somnolence (13%
vs 6%), dizziness (12% vs 7%), tremor (11% vs 3%) and sexual dysfunction.
Serious risks include hyponatremia/SIADH, increased bleeding, serotonin
syndrome, and QT prolongation in postmarketing and overdose reports. The
boxed warning for suicidality applies mainly to patients under 25; in
patients 65 and older, antidepressants were associated with fewer
suicidality cases than placebo.  

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Plasma clearance is about 40% lower in elderly patients than in younger
adults, and steady state may take 2-3 weeks. The general terminal
half-life is about 26 hours. Elderly patients are at greater risk of
hyponatremia.  

## Dosing Considerations

The usual starting dose is 50 mg once daily, with a maximum of 200 mg/day
and dose changes at least 1 week apart. In older adults, starting at 25 mg
once daily is common. In mild hepatic impairment, exposure is about 3
times higher, so use a lower or less frequent dose. No renal adjustment is
needed. Taper rather than stopping abruptly.   

## Interactions

MAOIs and pimozide are contraindicated. NSAIDs (ibuprofen), aspirin,
warfarin and other anticoagulants such as apixaban increase bleeding risk;
sertraline raised prothrombin time by 8% in patients on warfarin. Tramadol,
triptans, linezolid, lithium and St. John's wort increase serotonin
syndrome risk. Sertraline inhibits CYP2D6, so TCAs such as amitriptyline
and other narrow-range CYP2D6 substrates may need lower doses. Diuretics
add to hyponatremia risk. Sertraline also raised zolpidem levels by 43%.
 

## Monitoring

Check sodium at baseline and within the first few weeks in older adults,
especially those on diuretics or volume-depleted, and again if confusion,
weakness or unsteadiness develops. Monitor for bleeding in patients on
anticoagulants, antiplatelets or NSAIDs, and for falls. Watch for mood
changes and suicidality early in treatment and after dose changes.
  

## Alternatives

If hyponatremia or bleeding is a problem, mirtazapine is a common
alternative in older adults, with lower hyponatremia and bleeding risk,
though it causes sedation and weight gain. Bupropion avoids serotonergic
bleeding risk but lowers the seizure threshold. Psychotherapy should be
considered alongside or instead of drug therapy. 

## Urgent Signs

Severe hyponatremia (sodium below 120 mmol/L, with cases below 110
reported) presents with confusion, weakness, unsteadiness, seizures and
coma, and can be fatal. Serotonin syndrome (agitation, high fever,
sweating, tremor, muscle rigidity, diarrhea), especially in a patient also
on tramadol, needs emergency care. Overdose causes somnolence, vomiting,
tachycardia, agitation, tremor, and less often QT prolongation, seizures
and coma. Management is supportive; there is no antidote.  


# Gabapentin

## Overview

Gabapentin is an anticonvulsant used for postherpetic neuralgia and as
adjunctive therapy for partial seizures, and widely used off-label for
other neuropathic pain. In older adults its main risks come from renal
accumulation: it is cleared entirely by the kidneys, so reduced kidney
function directly raises exposure and CNS side effects.  

## Mechanism

Gabapentin is structurally related to GABA but does not bind GABA
receptors or affect GABA metabolism. It binds the alpha-2-delta subunit
of voltage-gated calcium channels, which is thought to reduce excitatory
neurotransmitter release, though the exact mechanism of its analgesic and
anticonvulsant effects is unknown.  

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

In postherpetic neuralgia trials, common effects vs placebo were
dizziness (28% vs 7.5%), somnolence (21.4% vs 5.3%), peripheral edema
(8.3% vs 2.2%) and ataxia (3.3% vs 0%). In older patients, ataxia and
peripheral edema increased with age. Other risks include suicidal
thoughts, DRESS/multiorgan hypersensitivity, and withdrawal seizures if
stopped abruptly.  

Current FDA labeling also warns about serious respiratory depression when
gabapentin is combined with opioids or other CNS depressants, or used in
elderly patients or those with lung disease. 

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Gabapentin is not metabolized and is excreted unchanged in urine, with a
half-life of 5-7 hours in normal renal function. Clearance drops with age,
from about 225 mL/min under age 30 to about 125 mL/min over age 70. In
anuric patients the half-life is about 132 hours. In PHN trials, patients
75 and older had larger effects, likely from higher exposure due to
reduced renal function. 

## Dosing Considerations

For postherpetic neuralgia, start at 300 mg on day 1, 600 mg/day on day 2,
900 mg/day on day 3, then titrate to 1800 mg/day; no added benefit was
shown above 1800 mg/day. Doses must be adjusted by creatinine clearance:
- CrCl 60 or more: 900-3600 mg/day
- CrCl 30-59: 400-1400 mg/day, split twice daily
- CrCl 15-29: 200-700 mg/day, once daily
- CrCl below 15: 100-300 mg/day, once daily; reduce the daily dose in proportion to CrCl (for example, CrCl 7.5 gets half the daily dose of CrCl 15)
- Hemodialysis: maintenance dose by CrCl plus 125-350 mg after each 4 hours
of dialysis

Older adults should start at the low end. Taper over at least 1 week when
stopping. 

## Interactions

Opioids increase CNS depression; morphine raised gabapentin exposure by
44%. Other CNS depressants (diazepam, zolpidem, tramadol, sedating
antihistamines) add to sedation, dizziness and fall risk. Antacids with
aluminum or magnesium reduce absorption by about 20%, so gabapentin should
be taken at least 2 hours after them. It has no meaningful interactions
with other anticonvulsants and is not affected by CYP enzymes. 

## Monitoring

Check renal function at baseline and periodically, and re-dose when CrCl
changes. Monitor for dizziness, sedation, ataxia, falls, peripheral edema,
mood changes and, with opioids or other CNS depressants, breathing.


## Alternatives

For neuropathic pain in older adults, topical lidocaine or capsaicin for
localized pain avoids systemic CNS effects. If a systemic agent is needed,
duloxetine is an option for some patients but brings serotonergic and
hyponatremia risks; nortriptyline is an option with lower anticholinergic
burden than amitriptyline. 

## Urgent Signs

Overdoses up to 49 g caused double vision, slurred speech, drowsiness,
lethargy and diarrhea, and all patients recovered with supportive care.
Hemodialysis removes gabapentin and may be needed in significant overdose
or renal impairment. Excessive sedation, confusion or slowed breathing in
a patient with declining kidney function or on opioids needs urgent
evaluation. 


# Loratadine

## Overview

Loratadine (Claritin) is a second-generation "non-sedating" antihistamine
used for hay fever and other upper respiratory allergy symptoms: runny
nose, sneezing, and itchy or watery eyes and throat. It is available over
the counter and is the recommended alternative to diphenhydramine in older
adults because of its much lower anticholinergic and sedative burden.


## Mechanism

Loratadine is a selective inverse agonist at peripheral histamine H1
receptors. It crosses the blood-brain barrier much less than
first-generation antihistamines like diphenhydramine, which is why it
causes less sedation. Unlike diphenhydramine, it is largely free of
antimuscarinic effects. Its active metabolite desloratadine is responsible
for most of the antihistamine effect. 

## Adverse Drug Reaction Profile

Common effects are headache, sleepiness and dry mouth. Taking more than
directed may cause drowsiness. Serious effects are rare and include
allergic reactions, seizures and liver problems. It does not cause the
urinary retention, constipation, blurred vision or delirium seen with
diphenhydramine. 

## Geriatric Pharmacokinetics

Loratadine is well absorbed and undergoes extensive first-pass hepatic
metabolism, mainly by CYP3A4 and CYP2D6. The half-life is about 8 hours
(range 3-20), while desloratadine's is about 27 hours (range 9-92). About
40% is excreted in urine and a similar amount in feces. No specific
elderly-versus-young comparison is given in these sources. 

## Dosing Considerations

The dose for adults and children 6 and older is 10 mg once daily, with no
more than 10 mg in 24 hours. No separate dose is given for adults 65 and
older. Patients with liver or kidney disease should ask a doctor, since a
different dose may be needed.  In significant hepatic or renal
impairment, 10 mg every other day is commonly used. 

## Interactions

CYP3A4 inhibitors such as ketoconazole, erythromycin and cimetidine
increase loratadine levels, but at 10 mg this did not cause clinically
significant QT prolongation. Antihistamines should be stopped 48 hours
before skin allergy testing. Alcohol and other CNS depressants may add to
drowsiness at higher doses. 

## Monitoring

Usually minimal. Ask about drowsiness, especially at higher-than-labeled
doses or with other sedating drugs, and check that the patient is not also
taking an OTC first-generation antihistamine (e.g. diphenhydramine in
combination sleep or cold products). 

## Alternatives

Other second-generation antihistamines, cetirizine (10 mg once daily) or
fexofenadine (180 mg once daily), are similar options. Cetirizine is a bit
more sedating, and fexofenadine needs renal dose adjustment. Intranasal
corticosteroids are effective for allergic rhinitis without systemic
antihistamine effects. 

## Urgent Signs

In case of overdose, get medical help or contact a Poison Control Center
right away.  Overdose typically causes drowsiness, tachycardia
and headache. Signs of a serious allergic reaction (swelling of the face
or throat, difficulty breathing) or a seizure need emergency care.
